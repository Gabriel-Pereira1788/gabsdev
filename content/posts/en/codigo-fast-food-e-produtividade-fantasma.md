Today I want to talk about the use of AI in software development. And no, this isn't another post about whatever "10x your brain" skill is trending this week.

I can't shake one idea: most code written with AI today is **"Fast Food"**. It's fast, it looks tasty, it kills your problem on the spot. And in the long run it does you real harm. I'm not blaming AI for this. I'm blaming whoever is sitting in the chair, using the tool.

## The numbers don't lie

Last year GitHub logged over 5 billion commits, with a forecast to pass 16 billion in 2026. That's a massive exponential jump. We're building more software than at any other point in the history of computing, and this exact period is where we see the most vulnerabilities, the worst performance problems, and quality that's visibly worse than before.

More volume, less solidity. Both showed up together, and that's not a coincidence: it comes from an old neglect of fundamentals and basics, with two roots.

The first is the post-pandemic generation of programmers. A wave of courses promised learning fast, easy, and making a lot of money, completely neglecting the fundamentals of computing. That flooded the market with people used to living in a bubble of tools, where the answer to any problem is installing the next package.

The second is the ship-fast culture that dominates a large part of companies. Delivery became the only metric that matters. Nobody asks whether the dev understood what they just committed, only whether they committed fast.

## AI is a mirror, not a crutch

AI shows up in this scenario, in a market that already rewarded fast delivery over understanding, and it's just a tool. But a tool with one particular trait: it doesn't turn you into someone else, it hands back who you already are, just at higher volume.

If you're pragmatic and have a solid foundation, AI becomes an extension of your reasoning. You ask it for a code skeleton and you already know, before reading the answer, whether it fits the architecture you designed. You argue trade-offs with it the way you would with a senior colleague. The result is recognizable: you ship in a day what used to take a week, without losing control of what's in your repository.

If you come from that wave of "solved it with a package I never bothered to open and understand", AI just swaps suppliers. It used to be Stack Overflow, a YouTube tutorial, whatever package was trending. Now it's the prompt. You copy the answer, paste it, it works right away, and you move to the next problem without understanding what happened along the way. The tool changed, the habit didn't.

**One important point to highlight is that AI doesn't create any monster. It amplifies whoever you already were before it existed.** Multiplying zero still gets you zero. Multiplying someone who already had a foundation gets you the ten-times-better professional every LinkedIn post promises.

## Impostor syndrome and endless FOMO

Standing on the wrong side of that mirror costs you, though. And then come two symptoms of whoever's in that second group. The first is impostor syndrome, an endless sea of FOMO where you chase the next tool that promises to change your life, the next hundred-dollar model that's going to think and code for you perfectly.

The second is what I'm calling ghost productivity. You write a lot more code, commit a lot more, juggle a lot more projects at once. And the quality of those projects is infinitely worse, because the pragmatism to stop, understand the problem, and pick the right tool never existed in the first place. A lot of volume, very little backing it up.

## Being an engineer again

This isn't a doomsday warning. It's my opinion on where we are: using the best productivity tool this field has ever seen to industrialize a habit that was already bad before it existed.

And, to be honest, the tool was never the problem. AI in the hands of someone who understands what they're doing is a real multiplier. The point is to act like an engineer, ask AI relevant questions, and command and architect the system without losing control of the code. That takes stepping back and studying what actually matters, instead of chasing whatever skill is trending this week.
